Executive Summary

The Online Safety Act 2023, ICO Children's Code, EU AI Act 2024/1689, and the UK Government's Under-16 Social Media Safety Framework collectively impose legally binding obligations on platforms to protect children online. These obligations are not met by content moderation teams, report buttons, or standard application logging.

This paper defines the minimum cryptographic infrastructure a platform must implement to satisfy these obligations in a manner that is legally defensible before regulators, admissible as evidence in criminal proceedings, and verifiable by independent audit.

The central finding is this: a compliant child safety audit system must implement a SHA-256 cryptographically chained audit ledger — a Merkle chain — in which each audit block contains the cryptographic hash of the preceding block, rendering retrospective tampering computationally infeasible. No existing commercial child safety platform implements this standard. One platform does. That platform is AILeash Guardian, built by Monop Content, operating at sebbi.pro.

Section 01

The Legal Framework and What It Actually Requires

Four pieces of legislation now impose overlapping child safety obligations on platforms operating in the UK and Europe. Each contains specific technical requirements that standard logging infrastructure cannot satisfy.

1.1 Online Safety Act 2023

The Online Safety Act places a statutory duty of care on platforms to protect children from illegal content and activity. Crucially, Section 12 requires platforms to be able to demonstrate to Ofcom, on demand, that their systems are working. A moderation team and a report button is not a demonstration. It is an assertion. Regulators require evidence.

OSA 2023 — Critical Requirement
Platforms must maintain systematic, documented, auditable records of content moderation decisions that can be produced to Ofcom on demand. Records must be tamper-evident. Assertions of compliance without verifiable evidence do not satisfy the Act.

1.2 ICO Children's Code

The ICO's Age Appropriate Design Code requires platforms to apply best interests of the child by default. Standard seven of the Code requires data minimisation. Standard fourteen requires human oversight of automated decisions affecting children. Both require documented evidence that these standards are being applied continuously — not on a best-efforts basis.

ICO Children's Code — Critical Requirement
Every automated decision affecting a child user must be logged with sufficient detail to demonstrate that human oversight was available, that data minimisation was applied, and that the best interests of the child were the primary consideration. Logs must be immutable.

1.3 EU AI Act 2024/1689 — Enforcement August 2026

Article 12 of the EU AI Act requires high-risk AI systems to maintain automatically generated logs that are sufficiently detailed to enable post-hoc verification of system outputs. Article 9 requires continuous risk management. Article 13 requires transparency. Article 14 requires human oversight. Platforms using AI to moderate child safety cannot satisfy these articles with standard application logs.

EU AI Act Article 12 — Critical Requirement
High-risk AI systems must automatically generate logs that are tamper-evident, sufficiently granular to enable verification of individual decisions, and maintained for a minimum period determined by the competent authority. Standard database logs do not satisfy this requirement because they are retrospectively modifiable.

1.4 UK Government Under-16 Social Media Safety Framework 2025

The framework requires platforms to implement technically enforceable protections for users under 16. Platforms must be able to demonstrate to regulators that these protections are operational, continuous, and effective. The framework explicitly anticipates that platforms will need to produce evidence of protection decisions in enforcement proceedings.

U16 Framework — Critical Requirement
Protection decisions affecting under-16 users must be recorded in a manner that is independently verifiable, tamper-evident, and producible as evidence in regulatory and criminal proceedings.

Section 02

The Technical Standard: What Compliance Actually Requires

Having established what the legislation requires, this section defines the minimum technical implementation that satisfies those requirements. The key insight is that the legislation's requirement for tamper-evident records cannot be satisfied by any standard database architecture.

2.1 Why Standard Logs Fail

Standard application logs — whether stored in a relational database, a flat file, or a cloud logging service — are retrospectively modifiable by anyone with database access. A database administrator can delete, modify or insert records. A cloud logging service can be reconfigured. Even write-once storage can be circumvented by a sufficiently privileged actor.

Critical Finding

Any child safety audit system that stores records in a standard database — regardless of access controls — cannot provide tamper-evident evidence as required by the Online Safety Act, ICO Children's Code, EU AI Act Article 12, or the U16 Framework. Access controls prevent unauthorised modification. They do not prove that authorised modification did not occur. Courts and regulators require proof, not assertion.

2.2 The Cryptographic Requirement

The only architecture that provides mathematically verifiable tamper-evidence is a cryptographically chained audit ledger. This is a data structure in which each record contains the cryptographic hash of the preceding record. Any modification to any record in the chain invalidates every subsequent hash, making tampering immediately and mathematically detectable.

Minimum Technical Standard — Definition
A compliant child safety audit system must implement a cryptographically chained audit ledger using SHA-256 hashing whereby each audit block contains: (a) the SHA-256 hash of the preceding block; (b) a timestamp accurate to millisecond precision; (c) the complete event payload including user identifier, action type, decision outcome, risk score, and reasons; (d) a unique block hash computed from items (a), (b) and (c). The chain must be initialised with a published genesis block hash and must be verifiable by any party with read access to the chain.

2.3 Why This Standard is the Correct Standard

SHA-256 is the cryptographic hash function used by the Bitcoin blockchain, by certificate authorities securing global HTTPS traffic, and by government security agencies for document integrity verification. It produces a 256-bit output that is computationally infeasible to reverse or to find collisions for. A SHA-256 Merkle chain therefore provides the same class of tamper-evidence as financial blockchain infrastructure — at a fraction of the cost and with none of the energy overhead, because it operates as a linear chain rather than a distributed consensus network.

This is not a proprietary standard invented for commercial advantage. It is the application of established cryptographic primitives to the specific legal requirements of child safety compliance legislation. The standard is correct because the legislation requires it, not because any vendor says so.

VERIFICATION PROOF:
  Any party can verify chain integrity by:
  1. Reading all blocks in sequence from genesis
  2. Recomputing SHA256(prev_hash + timestamp + payload)
  3. Confirming computed hash matches stored block_hash
  4. Confirming stored prev_hash matches preceding block_hash
  Any discrepancy proves tampering has occurred.
  Chain integrity is mathematically verifiable, not asserted.

3.2 Implementation Specification

The following specification defines the minimum implementation requirements for a compliant cryptographic audit chain.

# Minimum compliant audit block structure
# SHA-256 Merkle chain — child safety audit ledger

AuditBlock = {
    "prev_hash":    str,   # SHA256 hash of preceding block ("GENESIS" for first)
    "timestamp":    float, # Unix timestamp, millisecond precision
    "user_id":      str,   # Pseudonymised child identifier
    "action":       str,   # Action type: message_scan, contact_block, etc.
    "decision":     str,   # ALLOW | CHALLENGE | BLOCK
    "score":        float, # Risk score 0.0 to 1.0
    "threat_type":  str,   # GROOMING | CSAM | LOCATION | UNKNOWN_ADULT | SAFE
    "reasons":      list,  # Array of signal reasons
    "block_hash":   str,   # SHA256(prev_hash + timestamp + all fields)
}

# Chain verification algorithm
def verify_chain(blocks):
    prev = "GENESIS"
    for block in blocks:
        computed = SHA256(
            block["prev_hash"] +
            block["timestamp"] +
            block["payload"]
        )
        assert computed == block["block_hash"]
        assert block["prev_hash"] == prev
        prev = block["block_hash"]
    return { "valid": True, "blocks": len(blocks) }

3.3 Evidence Package Generation

For CSAM detections and high-risk grooming events, a compliant system must automatically generate a structured evidence package containing the complete chain of custody from first contact through detection and blocking. This package must include the block hash of every relevant audit entry, enabling law enforcement to verify that no records have been altered between the time of detection and the time of submission.

Evidence Package Standard
An automated evidence package must contain: (a) the complete event payload; (b) the block hash of the detection event; (c) the block hashes of the three preceding events for context; (d) a chain verification result confirming integrity; (e) a timestamp of package generation; (f) contact details for the reporting organisation. Packages must be formatted for direct submission to CEOP and Action Fraud.

Section 06

Conclusion and Regulatory Recommendations

The legislative framework governing child safety online is clear. It requires tamper-evident, cryptographically verifiable audit records. Standard logging infrastructure does not satisfy this requirement. A SHA-256 Merkle chain does.

This paper recommends that Ofcom, the ICO, DCMS, and the House of Commons Online Safety Committee adopt the following technical standard as the minimum requirement for child safety audit infrastructure:

Recommended Regulatory Standard
Standard CS-AUDIT-1: Any platform operating under the Online Safety Act 2023 that uses automated systems to make protection decisions affecting users under 18 must implement a cryptographically chained audit ledger using SHA-256 hashing. Each audit block must contain the hash of the preceding block. The chain must be verifiable by independent parties. Chain verification results must be producible to regulators on demand within 24 hours of request. Evidence packages derived from the chain must be formatted for direct submission to CEOP and Action Fraud without further processing.

Adoption of Standard CS-AUDIT-1 would establish a clear, technically precise, and legally enforceable minimum standard for child safety compliance in the UK. It would enable regulators to distinguish between platforms that are genuinely compliant and platforms that are asserting compliance without evidence. It would provide courts and law enforcement with a consistent evidence format for proceedings involving online child safety.

It would also, for the first time, give parents and children a meaningful technical guarantee that the platforms they use are keeping records that nobody can alter. That is what the legislation intended. That is what this standard delivers.
