What the law actually protects your child from online

The Online Safety Act gives platforms real legal duties to protect children. Most parents have never seen a plain-English version of what that actually means. Here it is.

The short version

The Online Safety Act 2023 puts legal duties of care on social media, gaming platforms, and search services used by children in the UK. These aren't guidelines — they're enforceable by Ofcom, with fines of up to £18 million or 10% of a company's global turnover, whichever is higher.

That means the platforms your child uses are legally required to assess risks to children and put safeguards in place — not simply respond after something goes wrong.

What's already in force

March 2025
Illegal content duties

Platforms became legally required to protect users from illegal content, with fast-acting removal processes.

July 2025
Children's safety duties

Platforms likely to be accessed by children must now use highly effective age checks and block content promoting self-harm, suicide, or eating disorders.

2026
Full enforcement underway

Ofcom has moved to measurable compliance outcomes and is actively investigating platforms — including formal information requests to dozens of services this year.

Ongoing 2026
AI chatbots & gaming platforms under review

A government consultation is underway on further protections for children on social media, gaming platforms, and AI chatbots — including a possible minimum age for social use.

What you can actually ask a platform to do

Age checks that work

Platforms must use "highly effective" age assurance — not just a tick-box — to keep children away from adult content and content promoting self-harm.

A working complaints process

Every regulated platform must have a clear, fast complaints procedure. If you report something, it's a legal duty, not a courtesy.

Annual risk assessments

Platforms likely used by children must assess and document harms at least yearly — and be ready to show Ofcom, on request.

The right to ask, if the worst happens

New guidance is being finalised on what bereaved parents can request about a child's account and activity, handled with dignity.

None of this replaces you. Ofcom itself is clear: technical controls matter, but the strongest protection is still a child who feels able to come to you when something's gone wrong online.
